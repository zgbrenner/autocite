Law-review citation checking is not one task. It is a sequence of editorial, source-verification, and formatting decisions that should remain distinguishable throughout the edit.

A useful tool can reduce repetitive mechanics and improve the audit trail, but it should not collapse source pulling, proposition review, quotation checking, and citation formatting into one opaque "fixed" result.

## 1. Preserve the submitted version

Keep the author's original draft unchanged. Create a separate working copy and record the document version used for the citation review.

This matters because footnote numbering, cross-references, quotations, and short forms can shift during substantive edits. A citation issue found against one revision may no longer point to the same text later.

## 2. Identify the controlling style sources

Before editing, collect:

- the journal's style guide;
- the applicable Bluebook or other citation manual;
- source-pull and archival requirements;
- abbreviation and typography preferences;
- rules for author queries and unresolved sources.

A generic Whitepages workflow is only the starting point. Journal-specific rules control the final publication.

## 3. Inventory every authority

Create or generate an inventory of citations and source types. Track cases, statutes, regulations, books, articles, reports, websites, archival sources, and any unusual material.

A document-wide inventory helps identify:

- repeated full citations that should use short forms;
- short forms with no clear antecedent;
- inconsistent source names;
- duplicated sources with different metadata;
- source categories that need specialized review.

## 4. Resolve short forms conservatively

Review `Id.`, `supra`, `supra note`, shortened case names, and hereinafter references against the full document. Do not assume that the closest visible citation is the intended source when a footnote contains multiple authorities.

AutoCite's citation graph can surface candidate relationships, but an editor should confirm ambiguous cases and follow the journal's rule.

## 5. Pull and verify the sources

For each authority:

1. obtain an approved source;
2. confirm title, author, court, reporter, year, publication information, URL, and archival data as applicable;
3. open the pinpoint location;
4. compare quotations character by character under the journal's policy;
5. evaluate whether the source supports the proposition;
6. record unresolved questions for the author or supervising editor.

A source that contains similar language may still fail to support the proposition in context.

## 6. Separate mechanical edits from source judgments

Mechanical changes include supported abbreviation, punctuation, spacing, or typography corrections that do not introduce new citation facts. Source judgments include deciding which authority is intended, whether a quotation is fair, or what proposition a source supports.

Keeping those categories separate makes review safer and makes author queries clearer.

## 7. Re-run after substantive changes

Citation review should not be a one-time pass. Re-run the mechanical and relationship checks after footnotes are moved, authorities are added, or prose is revised.

Exact-span validation is useful here: a stale issue should fail rather than apply its correction to unrelated text.

## 8. Perform the final proof in publication context

Before publication, review the formatted document or proofs for:

- footnote numbering and cross-references;
- line and page breaks;
- typeface and small-cap treatment;
- quotation marks and block quotations;
- URLs and archival links;
- tables, appendices, and captions;
- journal-specific conventions.

A document parser or DOCX exporter may preserve text-level changes without preserving every advanced Word field, style, or pagination choice. Final proofing remains necessary.
