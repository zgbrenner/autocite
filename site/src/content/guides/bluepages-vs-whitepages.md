The first citation question is often not how to format a source. It is **which citation system the document is supposed to follow**.

The Bluebook separates practitioner documents from academic legal writing. AutoCite calls these workflows **Bluepages** and **Whitepages** because the distinction affects citation form, typeface conventions, signals, short forms, and editorial expectations.

## Use Bluepages for practitioner documents

Bluepages are generally the better starting point for documents filed with a court or written for legal practice, including:

- briefs and motions;
- pleadings and declarations;
- practitioner memoranda;
- client-facing legal analysis;
- judicial chambers work when the court follows practitioner conventions.

A filing may also be governed by local court rules, standing orders, judge-specific preferences, or a jurisdiction's citation manual. Those authorities control when they differ from a general Bluebook convention.

## Use Whitepages for academic legal writing

Whitepages are generally the better starting point for:

- law-review articles;
- student notes and comments;
- seminar papers;
- academic books and chapters;
- journal editing and source pulls.

Academic work tends to use more footnotes, more source categories, and more complex short-form relationships. Journal-specific style guides often add another layer of requirements.

## Why the choice matters

The same authority can appear differently depending on the document. The difference is not merely cosmetic. A citation checker needs to know whether it is reviewing a filing, an office memorandum, or a footnoted article before it can prioritize the right issue families.

AutoCite can detect a likely mode from document structure and citation patterns, but the reviewer should confirm the selection. A long document with footnotes is not automatically a law-review article, and a short academic response is not automatically a practitioner filing.

## A practical decision rule

Ask three questions:

1. **Who will receive the document?** A court, client, supervising attorney, professor, or journal?
2. **Where do the citations appear?** In the body, in footnotes, or in both?
3. **What authority controls the format?** Court rules, assignment instructions, journal rules, or the general Bluebook system?

When the controlling instructions are unclear, use the document's intended audience as the starting point and confirm before final submission.

## What a citation checker cannot decide for you

A tool can identify citation-shaped text, compare it with supported mechanical rules, and flag ambiguous short forms. It cannot decide which local convention a judge, professor, or journal expects unless that convention has been supplied and verified.

AutoCite therefore publishes its rule coverage and labels generic jurisdiction profiles as requiring current local-rule review. A "clean" result means no remaining issue detected within supported checks, not universal compliance with every controlling instruction.

## A safer review workflow

1. Finish the substantive draft.
2. Confirm Bluepages or Whitepages mode.
3. Run a full-document citation review.
4. Accept supported mechanical fixes selectively.
5. Open every authority needed for quotation, pincite, and proposition review.
6. Apply controlling local, assignment, or journal rules.
7. Export a reviewed copy and keep the original.
