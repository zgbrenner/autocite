## 1. Introduction and Structural Overview

The 22nd Edition of *The Bluebook*, released in May 2025, represents the most substantial update to legal citation rules in over a decade. It introduces entirely new rules for tribal materials, archival sources, and artificial intelligence, while strictly tightening the requirements for citing digital media. [1] [2]

The book is divided into three primary sections:
1. **The Bluepages (B1–B23):** A practical guide designed for practitioners, law clerks, and paralegals preparing court documents and legal memoranda. It adapts the full rules into a condensed, practice-ready format.
2. **The Whitepages (Rules 1–23):** The comprehensive rules of citation and style, geared primarily toward academic legal writing (such as law review articles and research papers).
3. **The Tables (T1–T16):** Jurisdiction-specific citation formats, official source preferences, and exhaustive abbreviation lists. [3]

## 2. The Bluepages: Practitioner Rules (B1–B23)

The Bluepages instruct practitioners on how to adapt the Whitepages rules for court filings. The primary differences lie in typeface conventions (practitioners use standard roman and italics, avoiding the LARGE AND SMALL CAPITALS required in academic writing).

### B1–B9: General Rules
*   **B1 (Structure and Use of Citations):** Explains citation sentences, clauses, and signals.
*   **B2 (Typeface):** Dictates the use of italics (or underlining) for case names, book titles, and article titles in practitioner documents.
*   **B3 (Subdivisions):** Covers pages, footnotes, and paragraphs.
*   **B4 (Short Citation Forms):** Prohibits the use of *supra* and *hereinafter* for legislative debates. [4]
*   **B5 (Quotations):** Permits the parenthetical "(citation modified)" when a quotation has been stripped of internal marks to enhance readability. The trendy "(cleaned up)" parenthetical is explicitly rejected. [2] [4]
*   **B6 (Abbreviations):** General abbreviation principles.
*   **B7 (Italicization):** Rules for italicizing signals and explanatory phrases.
*   **B8 (Capitalization):** Standard capitalization rules.
*   **B9 (Titles):** Formatting titles of judges and officials.

### B10–B21: Source-Specific Rules
*   **B10 (Cases):** Provides precise lists of abbreviations allowed in case names in textual sentences versus citation sentences. [4]
*   **B11 (Constitutions):** Basic constitutional citation formats.
*   **B12 (Statutes):** Rules for citing federal and state codes.
*   **B13 (Legislative Materials):** Formatting for hearings, reports, and bills.
*   **B14 (Administrative Materials):** Explicitly addresses state regulation citation format, referencing Table T1.3. [4]
*   **B15 (Books & Non-Periodic Materials):** Requires listing up to three authors. Adds special forms for *Moore's Federal Practice* and *Wright & Miller*. [4]
*   **B16 (Periodical Materials):** Journal and magazine citations.
*   **B17 (Court Documents):** Broadens language to "any electronic docket number" (not just ECF), expanding applicability to state court systems. [4]
*   **B18 (The Internet):** Requires abbreviating main page titles per T6 and T10. Mandates a 24-hour clock and time zone for specific update times. [4]
*   **B19 (Services):** Looseleaf citations.
*   **B20 (Foreign Materials):** Basic practitioner formats for foreign law.
*   **B21 (International Materials):** Basic practitioner formats for treaties.

### B22–B23: New Additions
*   **B22 (Tribal Materials):** Mirrors Whitepages Rule 22; advises practitioners to follow specific Tribal Nation rules if submitting documents directly to that Nation's courts. [4]
*   **B23 (Archival Sources):** Provides a basic rubric for citing archival sources in practice, referencing Whitepages Rule 23 for details. [4]

## 3. The Whitepages: Academic Rules (Rules 1–23)

The Whitepages provide the exhaustive rules for academic legal writing.

### Rules 1–9: Structure, Use, and Style
*   **Rule 1 (Structure & Signals):** 
    *   **New Signal:** Rule 1.2 splits the former "Compare" signal into two. "Compare" is used when sources are similar; the new signal **"Contrast"** is used when sources are different in important respects. [4]
    *   **Parentheticals:** Now explicitly required for *see also*, *cf.*, and *see generally*. [5]
    *   **Pincites:** Required for all signals except *see generally*. [5]
*   **Rule 2 (Typography):** Re-titled from "Typefaces" to "Typography." Case names comprising an entire article title are not italicized. Ellipses and brackets in quoted text must be in roman type. [5]
*   **Rule 3 (Subdivisions):** Covers volumes, pages, footnotes, sections (§), and paragraphs (¶).
*   **Rule 4 (Short Citation Forms):** Covers *Id.*, *supra*, and *hereinafter*.
*   **Rule 5 (Quotations):** Block quotations (50+ words) must be "single spaced, fully justified." Double spacing is banned unless a monospaced font is used. [5]
*   **Rule 6 (Abbreviations):** Rules for spacing, periods, numerals, and symbols.
*   **Rule 7 (Italicization):** Do not italicize text from languages that do not use the Roman alphabet (e.g., Mandarin, Arabic). Do not italicize mathematical expressions generated by specialized programming languages like LaTeX. [5]
*   **Rule 8 (Capitalization):** Adds new guidance on capitalizing authors' last names exactly as they appear in the original publication. [5]
*   **Rule 9 (Titles):** Covers titles of judges, officials, and terms of court.

### Rules 10–17: Primary and Secondary Sources
*   **Rule 10 (Cases):** 
    *   Older cases using an inverted comma in party names (e.g., *M‘Culloch*) must use a "turned comma" (ʻ) rather than an apostrophe. [6]
    *   "LX" is now an approved short form for LexisNexis. [6]
    *   The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court is abbreviated "F.I.S.C." [6]
    *   If a case name is stated in full in the text, it does not need to be repeated in the footnote citation. [6]
*   **Rule 11 (Constitutions):** Foreign and Tribal constitutions now supersede Rule 11 and follow Rules 20.4 and 22.2.1, respectively. [6]
*   **Rule 12 (Statutes):** 
    *   Requires the publisher name before the database name for online statutes (e.g., West, Westlaw). [2]
    *   Codification parentheticals for session laws are now context-dependent. [6]
    *   The American Law Institute is abbreviated "A.L.I." [6]
*   **Rule 13 (Legislative Materials):** Clarifies abbreviation rules for individuals providing statements at federal hearings (using T6, T9, T11). [6]
*   **Rule 14 (Administrative Materials):** Adds a new subsection (14.4) dedicated entirely to state administrative materials, providing clarity on state rules, reports, and adjudications. [6]
*   **Rule 15 (Books & Reports):** 
    *   Citations must now list up to **three** authors before resorting to "et al." [6]
    *   Adds guidance for authors using pen names. [6]
    *   Permits the inclusion of an author's tribal affiliation in parentheses (e.g., *Ned Blackhawk (Western Shoshone)*). [6]
*   **Rule 16 (Periodical Materials):** Permits the inclusion of Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) in brackets at the end of periodical citations. [6]
*   **Rule 17 (Unpublished Sources):** Scope is narrowed strictly to materials *never* scheduled for publication; historical unpublished materials now fall under Rule 23. [6]

### Rules 18–23: Electronic, Foreign, Tribal, and Archival Sources
*   **Rule 18 (The Internet & Electronic Media):** Substantially reworked from 9 to 12 subsections.
    *   **Mandatory Archiving (18.2.1(d)):** Every internet citation must now include a Perma.cc-style archive link or explicitly state that a PDF is "(on file with author)." The former "encouraged" language is gone. [2]
    *   **AI-Generated Content (18.3):** A brand new rule for citing large language models (like ChatGPT). It requires the model name, a permanent archive link (or PDF on file), and the date of the query. [2] [7]
    *   **Social Media (18.10):** Expanded rules for citing posts, tweets, and profiles. [7]
*   **Rule 19 (Services):** Applies to looseleafs and topical compilations.
*   **Rule 20 (Foreign Materials):** Provides guidance for citing primary and secondary sources from other countries.
*   **Rule 21 (International Materials):** *U.S. Treaties in Force* (U.S.T.I.F.) is now the preferred source to cite for bilateral treaties to which the U.S. is a party. [8]
*   **Rule 22 (Tribal Nations) [NEW]:** Provides comprehensive guidance for citing materials from sovereign Tribal Nations.
    *   **22.1:** If a tribe has an established citation format, writers must defer to it (parallel citations to generic formats are required). [9]
    *   **22.2:** For tribes without established formats, the rule provides a uniform structure for constitutions, codes, orders, cases, and treaties. [9]
*   **Rule 23 (Archival Sources) [NEW]:** Standardizes citations for historical documents, letters, manuscripts, and physical objects housed in archives.
    *   **General Format:** `[Author], [Optional Author Title], [Document Title], [pincite], [(date)], (on file with [Archive Owner], [Archival Collection], [Archival Location]).` [10]
    *   Includes specific subsections for pre-1900 newspapers, tangible objects, and databases reprinting primary sources. [10]
